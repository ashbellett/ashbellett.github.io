Wand and Wit

Wand and Wit is a spell duelling game I'm building: a third-person 3D multiplayer magic duel for 2 to 10 players in a stylised fantasy forest. Players lock on to each other and trade spells, and every spell has a visible wind-up and travel, so the defender can read it and answer with a block or a dodge. The last player standing wins.

It's written from scratch in Rust, with no game engine: about 155,000 lines of Rust across a shared simulation, an authoritative server, a client that renders with wgpu natively and on WebGPU in the browser, and a headless test harness. You can play it at wandandwit.com in Chrome on a computer: host a room, send the code to your friends, and start the match when everyone is ready. It's still in development.

Readable duels

Every attack is decided at the moment it lands, by what the defender did: dodged while it flew, it misses; blocking as it lands, it's blocked; otherwise it hits. Blocking and dodging draw on one shared stamina that recovers slowly, and a blocked spell that empties it breaks the guard and staggers the defender. A spell's cooldown starts short and only grows to its full length if the server confirms a hit, so a whiffed spell comes back sooner. Control doesn't chain: a player who has just been knocked down, lifted or frozen can't be put under more until they've had a moment to recover. Players can jump and fly, and blocking works in the air.

Below, the wizard casts ice shard at the witch. The wind-up gathers crystals round him, a glowing crack runs along the ground to her, shards erupt round her, and the hit lands with an impact frame on the caster's screen. A dodge any time before the shards smash would have made it miss.

Spells

There are 24 spells in four classes. Each player picks three primaries for Q, E and R, a secondary for F and a signature for Tab before the match, and keeps them for the match.

  • Primaries: fire bolt, ice shard, lightning strike, light beam, black hole, boulder throw, brandish weapon (a purple flaming greatsword with a three-hit combo), raise skeleton (a minion that charges the nearest enemy and bursts into bones) and heal.
  • Secondaries: teleport behind (always out of the target's sight), go invisible, fling (a black tentacle lashes the target 18 m across the map), mirror spell (sends spells back at their caster), transform enemy (into a chicken) and disable spell (the target's next spell goes up as a firework instead).
  • Signatures: summon meteor, earthquake, volcanic eruption, time stop, thunderstorm and tidal wave. Each has a minute's cooldown and isn't ready for the first 30 seconds.
  • Cosmetic: every minute the server grants a random living player change time, change light or change weather, which reshapes the world for everyone while it's held.

Spells are data: their timings, damage, knockback and cooldowns live in a data file, built from shared parts (projectiles, placed areas, launches, melee swings, summons, timed states and map-wide fields), and the native client reloads it while the game runs, so a spell can be tuned mid-duel. Wind-ups sit on a 0.2-second grid by class, so each class has a few cast lengths to learn. The numbers are checked against a set of written balance principles by a report that runs every spell through them. Some of the stranger spells are built on small tricks. Time stop freezes every other player by moving all of their timers forward by the length of the stop, so nothing else in the simulation has to know about it, and spells aimed at a frozen player hang in the air until it ends. The volcano's lava bombs are generated from the cast's id, so the server and every client agree on where each one lands without a message per bomb.

Impact frames

A hit freezes the caster's view while the duel carries on. The renderer holds the last finished image for a few frames and keeps compositing it through the post-processing stage, so the grade, the speed lines and the HUD stay live while the simulation underneath never pauses. Each spell has its own sequence of frames, built from three kinds of step: a colour grade (greyscale, sepia, inverted), a two-tone frame that splits every pixel into one of two colours by its brightness, and a flash that washes the world toward a colour. Speed lines converge on the point of impact, and the caster's camera shakes and punches in. The player who was hit sees a red frame of their own instead, and their camera stays still. A comfort setting softens the flashes and inversions into a low-saturation hold.

Characters and animation

There are ten characters to choose from: wizard, witch, monk, mystic, scholar, alchemist, warlock, fairy, necromancer and goddess, each with one of six wands. They're cosmetic only, and each player in a room wears a different one. The characters arrive as finished, textured models, and a headless Blender pipeline, written in Python, turns each one into something the game can animate, with no hand rigging:

  • Preparation: the model is joined into one mesh, checked for scale and facing, welded along its UV seams and measured for holes and self-intersections.
  • Skeleton: the joints are found from the mesh's silhouettes (the armpit fold, the crotch, the narrowest wrist and neck, each finger's tip), with Mixamo's proportions filling in what a silhouette can't show, and Mixamo's 65-bone skeleton is built on them. Calibrated on Mixamo's own character, every joint lands within 2.6 cm of Mixamo's. The arms are then turned onto one line, so every character rests exactly as the skeleton does.
  • Skinning: bone-heat weights, then corrections: each hand vertex keeps one finger, far-off bones lose their pull, hair follows only the head and neck, and a coat's hem is told apart from the leg underneath it, since the clothes and the body are one fused surface.
  • Sockets and hands: a socket in each palm where a weapon sits, and hand poses solved so every finger bends until it meets the grip.
  • Simulation regions and colliders: hair, hems, wings and hat tips are marked per character as data, and twelve capsules sized from the body keep them out of it.
  • Checks: the exported glTF file is read back with an independent reader and checked joint by joint, weight by weight, and the stress poses are measured for collapsing or stretching triangles. Every weapon is tried in both hands of every character, and textures are capped at 1024 pixels, or 512 for the browser.

Animation comes from motion-capture clips, retargeted onto each character by the game. A data file says which part of which clip plays for each action and each spell's cast. Casts are laid over the legs, so a player can cast on the run, gaits blend by direction and speed, and hits, falls and deaths play by the side they came from. Cloth and hair swing with the body's movement and the wind, from springs on the regions the pipeline marked. The skeleton that raise skeleton summons and the chicken that transform enemy turns players into come through the same pipeline; the chicken has a skeleton and walk cycle of its own.

Audio

There's no music: the sound is the combat, the bodies and the world. Like the visual effects, audio lives entirely in the client and is driven by the simulation's events, by the animation and by the world as it's drawn, so the server and the rules know nothing about it. Each event becomes a cue that names keys from the most to the least specific, such as this spell's release heard by its own caster, then this spell's release, and the sound bank plays the first one it has. A recorded sound replaces exactly the cues it's keyed for, and anything without a recording can fall back to a placeholder synthesised in code at start-up.

Every cue has a seat: sounds the local player made play in their head, a spell aimed at them gets its own warning, and everyone else's are placed where they happen and fade with distance. A bolt from the sky uses a longer range, so a strike on the edge of the map is still heard. Each character speaks their incantations in one of two recorded voices, timed so the last word lands with the release. Footsteps fall where the run cycle's feet touch down, thunder follows its lightning at the speed of sound, and the nature, wind, rain and river beds follow the weather and the distance to water. Five buses (ambience, foley, combat, voice and interface) mix through kira, and the world ducks under an impact frame and falls silent through a time stop. The recordings are mastered by a script to a loudness target per kind of sound, and the browser downloads them as one bundle once the game has started, playing the placeholders until they arrive.

Rendering

The renderer is my own, written on wgpu with eighteen WGSL shaders, and the same code runs on Metal natively and on WebGPU in the browser. Each frame, the game hands it one description of what to draw, and it runs a fixed list of passes over an HDR target: shadows, the sky, the lit scene, decals, effects and sprites, ambient occlusion, bloom, god rays, streaks and flare, tone mapping, anti-aliasing, then the screen effects and the HUD. Every pass and every effect has its own switch and its numbers in a data file, so each can be judged alone and tuned while the game runs. The window and the headless captures share every pipeline, which is how every screenshot on this page was made.

Lighting and shadows

The world is cel-shaded: light falls off in steps, and shadows from cascaded shadow maps keep hard edges, with each cascade fitted to a slice of the view. Point lights from spells, wands and lanterns are clustered: the view is cut into a grid of tiles and depth slices, and each cell lists the lights that reach it, so a pixel only weighs the lights near it. Characters are shaded with their own normal, roughness and metal maps on top of the cel lighting, so trim catches the light and metal shines. Ambient occlusion reads as ink in the crevices.

Ink and outlines

Characters and props are outlined by inverted hulls, a slightly larger copy of each mesh drawn inside out in a dark ink colour. A screen-space ink pass outlines what the hulls miss, such as grass and leaves, by inking the near side of every jump in depth.

Sky, time and the moon

Matches start at golden hour, and the world clock is shared state that the server sends in every snapshot. Change time runs the clock forward for everyone while it's held, so a duel can carry on into the night. The sky, the sun, the ambient light, the fog and the lanterns all follow the hour. At night a moon rises on the sun's path, twelve hours behind it, with a phase that follows where the sun lights it from. As the sun sets, the moon takes over its shadows and its god rays. Change light pulls the sun and the moon nearer or pushes them farther away, cycling while it's held. The light falls off with the square of the distance, but stays within bounds so the world is never too bright or too dark to read a spell.

Fog and light in the air

Fog keeps the scene's colour rather than greying it out. A compute pass fills a grid of cells over the view each frame, marching out from the eye through the mist and lighting each cell by the sun through the shadow map, by the sky and by nearby lights; the scene and the sky read it back as a 3D texture. God rays, anamorphic streaks and lens flare come after, from the bright parts of the image.

Weather and water

The weather moves between sun, cloud, rain and storm, only ever to a neighbouring state, so the sky builds up and clears through cloud. Like the clock, it's state in every snapshot, so every player sees the same sky; the wind, the rain, the wetness and the storm's lightning are all worked out on each client from that state and never sent. It's cosmetic only: no rule reads it. Rain is thousands of strokes placed entirely in the vertex shader. A height map of the level, built once when the map loads, tells each drop where it lands, so no rain falls under a canopy or through a rock, and drops splash on the ground, on rocks and on the players. Open ground darkens as it soaks and puddles spread, then dry out after the rain.

The river is a fine mesh with waves driven by the wind. It refracts the scene beneath it, absorbs light with depth, and throws caustics on the bed. A wave equation stepped on the GPU carries ripples from waders, splashes, spells and rain, which cross each other and die on the bank.

Effects

Effects are a vocabulary of reusable pieces, each a data definition triggered by events from the simulation: wand trails, shockwave rings, dodge afterimages, ground cracks and flying rocks, two-tone smoke puffs with inked edges, flip-book explosions and hit sparks. Sprites and debris animate on twos, stepping at about 12 frames a second while the 3D scene renders at the display rate, as hand-drawn animation does. Spells light the scene round them, a block raises a shield of refracted light, and the black hole glows with a nebula and a corona and stretches the bodies it catches.

The world and the browser

Trees, rocks and ground cover fade between levels of detail with a dither, and the far forest is drawn as impostors. Vegetation, robes and hats sway in the wind in the vertex shader. Materials and character maps live in texture arrays, and a character's maps are written into them a few a frame as the characters load. On the high preset a frame takes about 8 ms at 1080p on an M4 Max; medium and low presets and a dynamic resolution that steps down when frames run over budget cover weaker machines. A frame profile times every pass on the GPU. The browser loads the game in steps: the page and the game itself, then the small map and the starting characters, then the rest of the characters and a room's map when they're needed.

The server and the simulation

The rules live in one pure simulation: a fixed 30 Hz tick, no rendering, no I/O and no clock, with time measured in ticks. The same code runs on the server, which decides every outcome, and in each client, which predicts its own player's movement, dodges and blocks the instant they're pressed. When a snapshot disagrees, the client rewinds to the server's state, replays its inputs and smooths away the difference. Everyone else is drawn a little in the past, interpolated between snapshots.

Spells aren't replicated as objects. The server sends one release event with the caster, the target and the tick it lands, and every client flies the spell from that. Hits favour the defender within a cap: a dodge counts if it was pressed before the spell landed, even if it arrived a little late. Clients reach the server through a websocket for signalling and WebRTC data channels for play, so the browser and native clients play together. Snapshots carry only what changed since the last one a client acknowledged, packed tight, which keeps a ten-player fight to 6 to 8 KB a second to each client; events are numbered and resent until they arrive.

Rooms are private and joined by a code. Each player picks a character and their spells, marks themselves ready, and the host begins; the map follows the number of players in the room, and loads only once every client reports ready. The dead spectate, and everyone returns to the room for a rematch. The page is served from Cloudflare, the game's files from R2, and the room servers run on a Vultr machine, each match writing a log of events that measure how it played.

A headless harness runs bot matches through the real server and client over a simulated network, at up to 300 ms of latency with jitter and 5% packet loss, and checks invariants on every tick: health never goes negative, cooldowns are respected, every match ends, nothing panics. A playtest goes further: whole sessions played by simulated people, from joining a room to the rematch, on the arena and every map, failing on any bug they run into. The workspace has about 1,360 tests.

Maps

Maps are generated ahead of time in three sizes, from 120 m across for two players to 280 m for ten, with hills, cliffs, rocks, a river to wade through and bushes to hide in, dressed from a kit of props. Each is baked once into the files the game loads, so every player gets exactly the same map, and rocks and tree trunks have colliders fitted to their meshes.
