Wand and Wit

Wand and Wit is a spell duelling game I'm building: a third-person 3D multiplayer magic duel for 2 to 10 players in a cel-shaded fantasy forest. Players lock on to each other and trade spells, and every spell has a visible wind-up and travel, so the defender can read it and answer with a block or a dodge. Land a hit and your screen plays an anime-style impact frame. The last player standing wins.

It's written from scratch in Rust, with no game engine: about 100,000 lines across a shared simulation, an authoritative server, a client that renders with wgpu natively and on WebGPU in Chrome, and a headless test harness. It's still in development.

Readable duels

Every attack is decided at the moment it lands, by what the defender did: dodged while it flew, it misses; blocking as it lands, it's blocked; otherwise it hits. Block and dodge are resources that recover slowly, and emptying a player's guard breaks it and staggers them. A spell's cooldown starts short and only grows to its full length if the server confirms a hit, so a whiffed spell comes back sooner. Players can jump and fly, and the whole fight works in the air.

Spells

There are 23 spells in four classes. Each player picks three primaries for Q, E and R, a secondary for F and a signature for Tab in the lobby, and keeps them for the match.

  • Primaries: fire bolt, ice shard, lightning strike, light beam, black hole, boulder throw, brandish weapon (a flaming sword with a three-hit combo), raise skeleton (a minion that hunts the nearest enemy) and heal.
  • Secondaries: teleport behind (always out of the target's sight), go invisible, fling (a giant finger flicks the target 25 m), mirror spell (sends spells back at their caster) and transform enemy (into a frog, chicken, sheep or pig).
  • Signatures: summon meteor, earthquake, volcanic eruption, time stop, thunderstorm and tidal wave. Each has a minute's cooldown and isn't ready for the first 30 seconds.
  • Cosmetic: every minute the server grants a random living player change time, change light or change weather, which reshapes the world for everyone while it's held.

Spells are data: their timings, damage, knockback and cooldowns live in a data file, and the native client reloads it while the game runs, so a spell can be tuned mid-duel. Some of the stranger ones are built on small tricks. Time stop freezes every other player by moving all of their timers forward by the length of the stop, so nothing else in the simulation has to know about it, and spells aimed at a frozen player hang in the air until it ends. The volcano's lava bombs are generated from the cast's id, so the server and every client agree on where each one lands without a message per bomb.

Impact frames

A hit freezes the caster's view while the duel carries on. The renderer holds the last finished image for a few frames and keeps compositing it through the post-processing stage, so the grade, the speed lines and the HUD stay live while the simulation underneath never pauses. Each spell has its own sequence of frames, built from three kinds of step: a colour grade (greyscale, sepia, inverted), a two-tone frame that splits every pixel into one of two colours by its brightness, and a flash that washes the world toward a colour. Speed lines converge on the point of impact, and the caster's camera shakes and punches in. The player who was hit sees a red frame of their own instead, and their camera stays still.

Rendering

The look is anime combat: smooth cel-shaded 3D with effects that read as hand-drawn. The renderer is my own, written on wgpu with fifteen WGSL shaders, and the same code runs on Metal natively and on WebGPU in the browser. It renders into an HDR target and tone-maps it, with stepped cel lighting, shadow maps whose edges stay hard, ambient occlusion that reads as ink in the crevices, bloom, fog, god rays, anamorphic streaks and lens flare. Characters and props get inked outlines from inverted hulls, and a screen-space ink pass outlines what the hulls miss, such as grass and leaves. Vegetation, robes and hats sway in the wind, and skirts are simulated as cloth.

Effects are a vocabulary of reusable pieces, each a data definition triggered by events from the simulation: wand trails, shockwave rings, dodge afterimages, ground cracks and flying rocks, two-tone smoke puffs with inked edges, flip-book explosions and hit sparks. Sprites and debris animate "on twos", stepping at about 12 frames a second while the 3D scene renders at the display rate, as hand-drawn animation does. Every effect and every rendering feature has its own switch and its numbers in a data file, so each can be judged on its own and tuned live. On the high preset a frame takes about 8 ms at 1080p on an M4 Max, and medium and low presets cover weaker machines.

Time, light and the moon

Matches start at golden hour, and the world clock is shared state that the server sends in every snapshot. Change time runs the clock forward for everyone while it's held, so a duel can carry on into the night. The sky, the sun, the ambient light, the fog and the lanterns all follow the hour. At night a moon rises on the sun's path, twelve hours behind it, with a phase that follows where the sun lights it from. As the sun sets, the moon takes over its shadows and its god rays.

Change light pulls the sun and the moon nearer or pushes them farther away, cycling while it's held. The light falls off with the square of the distance, but stays within bounds so the world is never too bright or too dark to read a spell.

Weather and water

The weather moves between sun, cloud, rain and storm, only ever to a neighbouring state, so the sky builds up and clears through cloud. Like the clock, it's state in every snapshot, so every player sees the same sky; the wind, the rain, the wetness and the storm's lightning are all worked out on each client from that state and never sent. It's cosmetic only: no rule reads it.

Rain is thousands of strokes placed entirely in the vertex shader. A height map of the level, built once when the map loads, tells each drop where it lands, so no rain falls under a canopy or through a rock, and drops splash on the ground, on rocks and on the players' hats. Open ground darkens as it soaks and puddles spread, then dry out after the rain. The river is a fine mesh with waves driven by the wind. It refracts the scene beneath it, absorbs light with depth, and throws caustics on the bed. A wave equation stepped on the GPU carries ripples from waders, splashes, spells and rain, which cross each other and die on the bank. Two signatures drive the weather too: thunderstorm turns it to a storm, and change weather steps it while it's held.

The server and the simulation

The rules live in one pure simulation: a fixed 30 Hz tick, no rendering, no I/O and no clock, with time measured in ticks. The same code runs on the server, which decides every outcome, and in each client, which predicts its own player's movement, dodges and blocks the instant they're pressed. When a snapshot disagrees, the client rewinds to the server's state, replays its inputs and smooths away the difference. Everyone else is drawn a little in the past, interpolated between snapshots.

Spells aren't replicated as objects. The server sends one release event with the caster, the target and the tick it lands, and every client flies the spell from that. Hits favour the defender within a cap: a dodge counts if it was pressed before the spell landed, even if it arrived a little late. Clients reach the server through a websocket for signalling and WebRTC data channels for play, so the browser and native clients play together. Snapshots carry only what changed since the last one a client acknowledged, quantised, which keeps a 1v1 to about 3 KB a second per player, and a ten-player match to under 9 KB.

A headless harness runs bot matches through the real server and client over a simulated network, at up to 300 ms of latency with jitter and 5% packet loss, and checks invariants on every tick: health never goes negative, cooldowns are respected, every match ends, nothing panics. The workspace has about 870 tests.

Maps and characters

Maps are generated ahead of time in three sizes, from 120 m across for two players to 280 m for ten, with hills, cliffs, rocks, a river to wade through and bushes to hide in. The host picks one in the lobby. Players build their wizard or witch in a character creator: body, build, hair, hat, robe and weapon, with their colours. The bodies come from MakeHuman and are fitted and baked in Blender ahead of time, and the look is purely cosmetic.
